Task Description

The evaluation of sex differences in the time course of physiologic responses and adaptations to the space environment is critical to ensuring the safety of crews as the world prepares for the first crewed spaceflights beyond low Earth orbit in 50 years and the first woman to step on the Moon. Microgravity during spaceflight damages musculoskeletal tissues, but the understanding of sex differences in musculoskeletal atrophy during spaceflight is incomplete. Maintaining musculoskeletal health during long-duration spaceflight is crucial for ensuring astronauts are physically ready for exploration class missions and mission success upon return to partial weightbearing activity. Due to the known links between sex and musculoskeletal health, it is critical to get a better understanding of “how” and “why” bone microarchitectural and material property changes occur over a mission duration and if that change occurs differently in males and females. Finite element (FE) modeling protocols and computational models have been developed to better identify how bone strength is compromised during reduced weight-bearing and other challenges. Preliminary data using FE modeling on female mice flow on Space Shuttle mission STS-135 indicated that proximal tibia bone density loss (-17%) underpredicted the attenuation in structural strength (-34%). These findings demonstrate a limitation in the current clinical assessments of bone health and fracture risk using dual energy x-ray absorptiometry (DXA), as well as the standard micro computed tomography (microCT) analysis for predicting bone health during spaceflight. The objective of the current proposal is to capitalize on samples from two completed projects that the team has already acquired through tissue sharing opportunities. The parent projects had male and/or female mice that underwent unloading (hindlimb unloading or spaceflight) for 14, 28, or 38 days (and appropriate controls). We will quantify bone cellular, microarchitecture, and strength changes using histology, microCT, computational FE modeling, and material property characterization. Successful completion of this project will provide foundational data for further mechanistic and translational studies examining sex differences in the musculoskeletal adaptation to spaceflight environmental stressors that will enable more specific countermeasure development.

Research Impact & Earth Benefits

The work is significant to NASA’s goal for maximizing astronaut health during long-duration spaceflight. The proposed work will provide critical insight on how changes in bone microarchitecture and material properties during spaceflight contribute to overall bone strength, which are important factors for understanding and mitigating fracture risk. The proposed work will synergize with the development of novel imaging modalities to monitor bone health and countermeasure development, which will benefit astronauts and people on Earth.

Task Progress Report

The proposed work uses a multi-scale approach to assess changes in bone health and strength in already available hindlimb bone specimens obtained from tissue sharing opportunities: 1) male mice from a 38-day International Space Station (ISS) flight study (80NSSC19K0392) and 2) male and female mice from a HU time course (14 vs 28 days) study (80NSSC19K1025). All live animal work was completed by July 2022 under approved Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) protocols at the parent study institutions. In the past reporting period, we have made progress toward the completion of SA1 and SA2, but have been delayed due to equipment issues and construction causing data variability issues.
