Task Description

We are developing a new diagnostic technology: magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and spectroscopy of hyperpolarized noble gases. Large non-equilibrium nuclear spin polarizations can be created in the spin-1/2 noble gases, 3He and 129Xe, using the technique of spin-exchange optical pumping. Such large polarizations greatly enhance the magnetic resonance detection of 3He and 129Xe, enabling high-resolution MRI studies of the lung and airways, as well as dissolved tissue-phase investigations of organs and tissues, such as the brain, using lipophilic 129Xe. An important feature of hyperpolarized noble gas MR is that it does not require a large magnetic field, unlike the conventional water proton MR method used in medical MRI. Low magnetic field MRI with hyperpolarized noble gases can allow us to use a small, light-weight, low-power devices. This capability provides potential for advances in both portable ground-based and practical space-based MR systems. Our proposed ground-based investigations with hyperpolarized noble gas MR will affect biomedical research in two important ways: (1) by providing the potential to acquire novel information about physiological structure and function of the lung and the brain in space-flight and in terrestrial gravity, and (2) by developing a new diagnostic technology that has clinical applications for diagnosis of pathology.

Research Impact & Earth Benefits

An important feature of hyperpolarized noble gas MR is that it does not require a large magnetic field, unlike the conventional thermally polarized water proton MR method used in medical MRI. As a result, at low magnetic fields (0.01 Tesla) the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) of hyperpolarized noble gas MR is much larger than the SNR of conventional thermally polarized MR. Low field MR utilizing hyperpolarized noble gases should be practical in a small, light-weight, low-power device, providing the potential for both portable ground-based and practical space-based MR systems. Specific magnetic and biological properties of hyperpolarized 3He and 129Xe MRI include: (i) airway and lung gas space imaging (for diagnosis of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), e.g. emphysema, asthma, and for studies of respiratory function); (ii) the imaging of lipid structures in the brain (for the diagnosis and staging of demyelinating diseases such as multiple sclerosis, and in research on brain function); and (iii) angiography and measurement of blood flow (for diagnosis of stroke and ischemia, and for research into the function of the brain and cardiopulmonary system). The recent progress made in our lab lays the foundation for the continued development of hyperpolarized xenon MRI for brain imaging. Specifically, imaging of lipid structures in the brain (for the diagnosis and staging of demyelinating diseases such as multiple sclerosis) and angiography and measurement of blood flow (for diagnosis of stroke and ischemia, and for research into the function of the brain and cardiopulmonary system), are now viable research goals. Because HP 129Xe is an MR signal source with properties very different from those generated from water-protons, it may yield structural and functional information not detectable by proton-based MRI methods. Thus brain imaging with hyperpolarized xenon should be of great benefit to the diagnosis and treatment monitoring of a number of neurological diseases.
